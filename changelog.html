What's new

This index started as a challenge. Jennifer Kleiman asked Cameron Byerley to name something an AI could reasonably contribute to math education, and Cameron proposed this one. AI agents have done the building and checking since then. Some of the biggest changes came from people who told us what we'd missed. Others came from the agents re-checking their own work.

October 30, 2026: needing training no longer keeps a product out

Cameron came across Project Aspire on What didn't make it. It's a teacher-knowledge instrument she worked on, and it was out because its rubrics need trained scorers. She asked who decided that. The rule was in our project plan: research instruments that need trained coders were out of scope. At the shortlist stage, our agents applied it to Project Aspire, Hill's Mathematical Quality of Instruction and Kersting's Classroom Video Analysis. Cameron pointed out that people can use the Aspire rubrics without training. Sometimes contacting the authors only means getting a password or agreeing not to share a video. Jennifer agreed and dropped the rule, on one condition: a teacher needs a way in. A product can now need training or a request to its authors, as long as the listing tells a teacher how to do it. All three instruments are reopened.

Jennifer also brought materials for college courses that prepare future K–12 math teachers into scope. Three products we had left out partly because they were written for teacher educators will be screened again: the TEACH Math learning modules, LessonSketch, and Preparing to Teach Mathematics with Technology. LessonSketch and Preparing to Teach Mathematics with Technology had other problems too, so they may stay out.

Illustrative Mathematics was still on What didn't make it, two days after it got its own listing. We've fixed that. On October 29 we said the double-counted money came to about $6 million. About $830,000 of that was on the TEACH Math modules, which aren't listed, so the listed products lost about $5.2 million. We've corrected that entry.

October 29, 2026: some NSF money was counted twice

When a research team moves to a new institution, NSF's records show a new award with the same title and lead investigator at the new place. On Instructional Nudges (Practice-Driven Professional Development), NSF's record for the old award, at the University of Missouri, showed $1,174,293. But its own year-by-year record showed only $23,768. The other $1,150,525 was what the new award at the University of Florida received that same year, so we had been counting it twice. Four more listings had the same problem: CODAP, Cognition-Based Assessment and Teaching, the FAACT fraction curriculum (where the money moved twice) and LEAP. So did the TEACH Math modules, which we track but don't list. In every case, the amount we had counted twice matched the new award's early years to within a dollar. Our totals now count only what each old award kept. That takes about $5.2 million off our NSF totals, all of it money that had been counted twice. One FAACT award now shows $0, because NSF's year-by-year record shows $0 for it.

October 28–29, 2026: we started counting

Until now we found products by starting from people and projects we knew about. That's how things get missed, so our agents began a census. They pulled the awards from NSF's education directorates whose record matched a search for "math" or "mathematics", 1975 to now: 37,612 records. On ten crowded days the database still wouldn't hand over everything, and 23 records are missing. Plain code, with no AI judgment, then dropped scholarships and fellowships, conferences, undergraduate-only work, awards where math comes up only in passing, and products already listed. That left 3,496 projects whose abstracts mention both math and K–12 schools or teachers. An AI model is now reading the 3,169 of those funded since 1990, looking for anything a teacher could use.

Counting turned up a surprise. When you page through a large search, NSF's public award database repeats some records and never shows others. One year-long search for 2015 came back with 1,456 records, but 92 were repeats, so 92 awards never appeared. Our harvester now splits every search into date windows small enough that one page holds them. A single day that still overflows gets paged three times and the results combined. The searches we ran before the census used the older method.

Cameron was at the NCTM conference on October 29, and two things came back from there.

  • Practice-Driven PD is now listed. Its library of "instructional nudges" for algebra teachers was recommended to Cameron at the conference by the project's own lead researcher, so the listing rests on its NSF funding, not on that recommendation. The nudges are small teaching moves, most with short videos and a PDF. NSF funded the project. Our agents had excluded it on October 26 after finding "no public release." Many of its PDFs were uploaded in November 2024, so this was another miss that someone else caught.
  • Someone who works on LEAP looked at our listing. They told Cameron the long-term goal is for LEAP not to be only a supplement, and that they weren't sure every lesson runs an hour. "Supplement" was Didax's word and "one hour" was Didax's and TERC's, but we had stated them as facts. The listing now says whose words they are. Following their suggestion, it also explains "equations" as statements that two expressions are equivalent.

We also fixed the spelling of Cameron's last name, which we had wrong on three pages. It's Byerley.

We set up a weekly check of the product, lesson and research links on every listing, starting November 2. Some publisher sites block automated checks, so a few links still need a person. We've also started asking the Internet Archive to save copies of product pages that it hasn't captured recently, a few at a time, so if a product disappears there will be a record of it. A policy for endorsements from math educators is in draft. An endorsement would count only if the endorser has no tie to the product: no role in making it, no money in it, no recent papers or grants with its authors, no advisor–advisee link with them, and no shared institution. Software would check those ties against public records, and people would make the judgment.

October 28, 2026: Jennifer found what our agents had passed over

Jennifer came across Amy Ellis and Rob Ely's Playful Mathematics activities, which our agents had passed over. They had checked one grant page, found nothing for teachers, and moved on. Playful Math is now listed. Our agents then went back over 18 of the products they had set aside for "no product we could find," searching harder this time. Seven came back, and six of those are now listed: Project AIM's Activating Math Talk, Project MATHEMATICS!, COMAP's Mathematics: Modeling Our World, the PCMI Teacher Program books, EDC's Young Mathematicians games, and NCTM's Problems of the Week. What didn't make it explains the rest.

The same day we found two bugs in our own checks. A bracketed "verify this" note our agents leave for themselves had ended up in a listing's text. And the validation step that was supposed to check every listing before publishing had been checking none of them. Both are fixed: the site now refuses to build if such a note would show, and validation checks every listing.

The index also grew past NSF. Illustrative Mathematics became the first listing with no NSF funding, admitted on independent, peer-reviewed research. Jennifer and Cameron also opened a second way in: a product can qualify if it is built on strong NSF-funded research, even when nobody has studied the product itself. In their view, studies of whether materials "work" are rarely sound, and research more often shows why a design is the way it is. No listing has come in that way yet.

You can now sort the browse page by date: the newest NSF award behind a product, or its most recent listed study.

October 27–28, 2026: Cameron's class tried it

Students in Cameron's math education class used the site. The search bar and some of the grade filters didn't work, and filtered rows weren't actually hiding. Those are fixed. The class also asked for more middle-grades material. Most listings were already for grades 6–8, but they were whole curricula, PD programs and platforms, and a teacher using an assigned curriculum couldn't see where any of them would fit. So listings now show a checked "Try one lesson": one specific lesson or task a teacher could use next week. Where we couldn't find a free lesson, the listing says so. There is also a "Tried it?" link for telling us how it went.

October 25–26, 2026: the first 55

Our agents built the first version starting on the evening of Sunday, October 25. One AI model drafted each listing from saved copies of its sources. A different model then checked every claim against the passage it came from, and only listings that passed went up. By early on October 26, 55 listings had each been checked twice.
