How we chose what to list

We list the strongest work from the field's strongest groups, not every NSF-funded product. Two AI agents built separate shortlists from opposite starting points. They agreed on 33 groups. A third agent ruled on every disagreement and settled on 45 groups and 59 products. A later search of PME-NA conference proceedings added two groups and three products. After drafting and checking, 55 products are listed.

Who picked the starting names?

Cameron Byerly, a math educator, named the first people and projects to look at: the Rational Number Project, Connected Mathematics, Pat Thompson, Marilyn Carlson, Deborah Hughes Hallett, Amy Ellis, Amy Hackenberg, Jessica Hunt and John Mason.

An agent searched NSF's public award database for each name as principal investigator or co-investigator. It found education awards for eight of the nine, from one award for Amy Hackenberg to 26 for the Connected Mathematics team. John Mason had none. The only match was a co-investigator with the same name on an unrelated mathematics and engineering scholarship award, so his materials can't meet our rule that every product traces to an NSF award.

Seeds were a starting point, not a guarantee. Here is where each one ended up:

  • Rational Number Project: listed (Initial Fraction Ideas).
  • Connected Mathematics: listed (Connected Mathematics 4).
  • Pat Thompson: listed through the free DIRACC calculus text. His teacher-knowledge instrument (Project Aspire) was left out because its rubrics need trained scorers.
  • Marilyn Carlson: listed (Pathways Precalculus).
  • Deborah Hughes Hallett: listed (the Calculus Consortium's Calculus: Single and Multivariable).
  • Jessica Hunt: listed (FAACT fraction curriculum). A second product, the ModelME game, is on hold until a person can test its teacher registration.
  • Amy Ellis and Amy Hackenberg: not listed. Both have strong NSF research records, but neither agent, nor the adjudicator on a second look, found a product a teacher could pick up and use.
  • John Mason: not listed, because no NSF award was found.

How did the two shortlist agents work?

The two agents ran independently and could not see each other's work. Each confirmed every award it used in NSF's award database and checked that each product's web page loaded.

Agent A (Claude Opus 5.5) started from the seeds and worked outward through people. It followed co-investigators on seed awards (James Fey led it to Core-Plus Mathematics, Eric Knuth to the LEAP early algebra project), then sister projects in the same NSF programs, then well-known NSF curriculum and professional development groups it proposed itself. It listed 37 groups, 43 flagship products and 174 awards, and set aside 19 candidates.

Agent B (Claude Fable 5.1) started from what teachers already use. It began with EdReports and What Works Clearinghouse reviews of NSF-origin curricula, NSF DRK-12 product lines (via CADRE), publisher catalogues (NCTM, Heinemann, TERC, EDC, WestEd) and the field's most-cited NSF research programs. Then it worked back to the awards. It listed 44 groups, 51 flagship products and 191 awards, and set aside 26 candidates.

Both agents scored each group from 1 to 5 on the same four things, in different order of emphasis:

  1. Sustained NSF funding for K–12 materials or teacher development.
  2. Research base. Agent A counted publications that NSF's own award records list, with extra weight for core math education and practitioner journals. Agent B also used WWC and EdReports reviews.
  3. Uptake and liveness: is the product in use and still available?
  4. How usable the flagship product is for a teacher.

Agent A gave ten groups a 5. Agent B gave seven.

Where did the two lists agree and differ?

The lists shared 33 of 48 groups. Every shared group got either the same score from both agents (21 groups) or scores one point apart (12 groups). Within the shared groups, the agents picked the same or compatible flagship products.

Four groups appeared only on A's list: Driscoll's geometric and algebraic thinking work at EDC, LessonSketch, MathTalk videos, and Pat Thompson's DIRACC calculus. Eleven appeared only on B's: Cognitive Tutor/MATHia, Introduction to Data Science, Cyberchase, Talk Moves, Math-Mapper 6-8, IMAP video cases, Fosnot's Contexts for Learning Mathematics, LOCUS statistics assessments, Math Teachers' Circles, the Problem-Solving Cycle, and Zoombinis.

Most one-list groups were ones the other agent's search path never reached, not ones it had rejected. That pattern fits the design: a people-first search finds research networks, and an uptake-first search finds widely used products and media.

How were disagreements settled?

A third agent (Claude Fable 5.1) read both lists and ruled on every group that appeared on only one list or got different scores, and logged a reason for each ruling. Where the lists gave different scores, it applied a fixed rule: 5 means strong on all four criteria, 4 strong on three, 3 strong on two. It kept 11 of the 15 one-list groups, cut 3, merged one into another group, added one group neither agent had kept, and changed 13 scores.

Five rulings show how this worked:

  • Interactive Mathematics Program was added even though both agents had left it out. Both had excluded it because they couldn't find a live publisher page. The adjudicator found Activate Learning's IMP page, which loaded with the four-year sequence, print and e-book options, and the line "Designed and field tested with support from the National Science Foundation." It confirmed three NSF awards for IMP and included it at a 4. Both agents had made the same mistake, which is the kind of shared error a second list can't catch.
  • 5 Practices for Orchestrating Productive Mathematics Discussions was dropped. Agent A had listed it as a flagship of the Smith and Stein group. The adjudicator found no NSF acknowledgment in the book listings, in the metadata of the 2008 article behind the book, or in the group's award abstracts. The group stayed, with its NSF-funded COMET casebooks as the flagship.
  • Building Blocks stayed through the shortlist and was cut after drafting. The adjudicator confirmed that NSF directly funded the curriculum (award 9730804 is titled "Building Blocks--Foundations for Mathematical Thinking, Pre-Kindergarten to Grade 2"). But when the drafting agent went to find it, the only edition on sale was McGraw Hill's 2025 PreK program for three- and four-year-olds, and no K–2 edition could be found. Since we cover K–12, the card was rejected. The same group's free Learning Trajectories site is listed.
  • Amy Ellis and Amy Hackenberg were checked again and stayed out. The adjudicator re-read the GAMMA-CAT project page, which lists no product, only the NSF award. The IDReAM resources page lists other people's videos, books and a GeoGebra app, plus a link to Lobato's MathTalk. Hackenberg's co-authored Math Recovery book isn't an NSF product. Ellis's research network still appears through LEAP and MathTalk.
  • Math-Mapper 6-8 and Contexts for Learning Mathematics came in. Agent A had excluded both because it couldn't confirm access. The adjudicator opened Math-Mapper's site in a browser and saw school and guest logins and free sample tests. For Contexts for Learning Mathematics, Heinemann's page said the series "is no longer for sale at Heinemann" and pointed to a new publisher, whose site sells full K–5 sets.

The adjudicator also cut Cyberchase's score from 4 to 3, on the grounds that its awards funded media production with evaluation rather than math education research. It left out LessonSketch because LessonSketch is built for teacher educators, and Math Teachers' Circles because its award studied an existing program rather than building teacher materials.

What did a search of PME-NA proceedings add?

It added three products to the shortlist. It also showed that most NSF awards that researchers credit in PME-NA papers haven't produced a product a teacher can use yet. PME-NA's yearly proceedings collect math education research papers, and their acknowledgments show which NSF awards the researchers themselves credit.

A separate scout agent (Claude Opus 5.5) downloaded ten years of PME-NA proceedings, 2016 to 2025, and pulled every seven-digit number printed near "National Science Foundation," "NSF" or an NSF division code. That gave 402 candidate award numbers, and 391 of them turned up in NSF's database. The scout kept the 300 that are K–12 math education awards. Then it compared them with the shortlist:

  • 38 were already on the shortlist.
  • 30 more belonged to a researcher already in a shortlisted group, under an award the group's entry didn't list.
  • 232 came from groups outside the shortlist.

The scout searched the web for products from the most-cited of those outside groups, about 25 of them, and proposed nine candidates. The adjudicator (Claude Fable 5.1) fetched every candidate page itself, confirmed all 15 of the candidates' awards in NSF's database, and applied the same inclusion rule as before.

Added (3):

  • NC2ML Discourse Moves kits, from North Carolina's VISIONS project. These are downloadable kits on nine high school discourse moves, each with a teacher guide, a PD slide deck and sentence-starter posters, and the kit files name the four NSF awards. This is a new group.
  • Animated Contrasting Cases in Geometry (AC2inG): free animated worked-example pairs for grade 8 geometry, with discussion questions and a teacher guide. The site credits NSF award 1907745. This is a new group.
  • M2Studio, a mathematical modeling environment from the Concord Consortium. It joins CODAP and TinkerPlots in an existing group.

Left out (6):

  • Math Habits Tool: a classroom observation instrument for coaches and researchers, not a teaching product.
  • M-DISC: a discourse PD curriculum that its publisher no longer sells.
  • TalkMoves app: sign-up requires a registration code, and nothing public says how a teacher gets one.
  • MCLE exemplar lessons: six lesson folders in a shared drive from a research study, too thin to stand beside the rest of the list.
  • COMPS/COMMIT-A word-problem tutor: no way to sign up, download or buy it.
  • MS-GGU graphing tasks: three tasks from a study that is still running. The adjudicator put them on a watch list.

Most of the groups PME-NA papers cite most often have no teacher product yet. That includes the MOST project, Orrill's proportional reasoning work, Choppin's online PD, Bofferding's integer research, Kosko's 360-degree video, and Kevin Moore's CAREER award, the single most-mentioned award. Their NSF work so far has produced research, not materials a teacher can pick up. MOST has a newer award to design a teacher learning sequence, and it is on the watch list.

The scout also found five award numbers printed in PME-NA papers that don't exist in NSF's database, probably typos.

What went wrong with old award numbers, and how was it fixed?

The first award searches missed NSF's oldest education awards. They kept only awards coded to NSF's education directorate (EDU or EHR), but education awards made before about 1985 carry a legacy code, CSE (division SED). Searches with that filter silently dropped the founding awards of some of the field's longest-running projects.

The orchestrating agent sent a correction, and both shortlist agents re-ran their searches to accept the legacy codes. Agent A's re-check added three legacy awards: two 1979 and 1981 awards to Merlyn Behr that started the Rational Number Project (7920591 and 8112643), and the 1981 Middle Grades Mathematics Project award (8018025) that preceded Connected Mathematics. Agent B's second pass, which also accepted education programs in other NSF directorates and small-business awards that name a product, added 28 award numbers, including those three and early awards for the Geometry Tutor and Sketchpad. No new group came in from the fix, but the funding histories now start where they really start. The earliest award on the site, 7920591, is one of the recovered ones.

What does a product need to be listed?

A product is listed only when all three of these hold:

  1. It traces to at least one NSF award number, confirmed in NSF's database.
  2. It exists and can be obtained today: free, by registration or by purchase.
  3. A teacher can use it without the research team present.

Curricula, units, single tasks, student assessments, teacher-knowledge instruments, videos, games, software and professional development materials are all in scope. Research instruments that need trained coders are out of scope, and so are papers with no usable product. Cost never keeps a product off the list, but each listing states it.

What are the limits of this list?

The list is curated, not complete. We set out to find the best work from the strongest groups. NSF has funded far more math education work than appears here, and the award database confirmed funding for the products we found. It wasn't used as a census.

Reputation signals favor well-known groups. Both agents leaned on uptake, citations, reviews and their own knowledge of which groups are prominent. Those signals favor large curriculum projects, research universities and products with marketing behind them. Smaller projects, curriculum developers who publish less, and newer work with little uptake yet are less likely to be found.

Some kinds of products are probably underrepresented:

  • Single lessons, tasks and small tools from projects that never became a curriculum or a branded product.
  • Work by small teams, regional partnerships and teacher-development projects whose materials live on a project page rather than with a publisher.
  • Recent awards whose products aren't widely known yet.
  • Products whose project sites have gone dark or that have no current publisher. That caused several exclusions, including SimCalc, Mathematics in Context and the National Library of Virtual Manipulatives.
  • Materials in languages other than English, and materials for students with disabilities or English learners. Neither agent's notes describe a search for these.

Every agent was a Claude model. The shortlist agents ran on two different models to reduce shared blind spots, but IMP shows that shared blind spots remain. What didn't make it lists every exclusion and its reason, so readers can judge the calls themselves.
