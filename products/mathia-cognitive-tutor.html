Before you use it

  • No price is posted; the product page offers a demo request. Samples sit in Carnegie Learning's Sample Center, which needs registration.
  • Every student needs a device and internet access. Supported devices are recent Windows and Mac computers, iPads (6th generation and later), Android tablets and Chromebooks with an 11.6-inch or larger display; phones are not recommended, and pop-up blockers must be off.
  • For your network, plan on no more than 25–30 computers per wireless access point and at least 1 Mbps of bandwidth for up to 30 students working at once.
  • Carnegie Learning lists MATHia under Supplemental Instruction and also calls it a key component of its Middle School and High School Math Solutions, so check whether you are buying it alone or as part of a program.
  • The pages we read don't state the course list, how much time students should spend each week or the standards alignment; ask Carnegie Learning.

What a teacher gets

MATHia is Carnegie Learning's adaptive math software for grades 6–12, with separate middle school and high school versions. Carnegie Learning markets it as a math supplement and also as a key component of its middle and high school math programs. Carnegie Learning describes it as the next generation of Cognitive Tutor, the high school tutor Carnegie Mellon researchers launched with Pittsburgh Public Schools in 1998.

Students work through workspaces of two kinds. Concept Builder workspaces use explore tools, animations, classification tools and worked examples, with a fixed number of problems. Mastery workspaces keep giving problems until MATHia estimates the student has mastered every skill, or until a maximum number of problems is reached. Students get hints on request and automatic hints after repeated errors, and see their skill estimates on progress rings. Teachers get Session, Skills, Student Detail and Standards reports, an end-of-year projection report, and LiveLab, which shows in real time who is working or idle and who needs help.

How these awards connect to this product

NSF funded the earlier Cognitive Tutor research at Carnegie Mellon; no Carnegie Learning page we saved names an NSF award or ties one to MATHia. The connection is inferred from three sources. NSF award 8751890 (John Anderson, Carnegie Mellon, 1988-1994) funded intelligent tutors in algebra and geometry, and 9253161 (Anderson, 1992-1998) funded an Algebra I tutor for use in the Pittsburgh Public Schools. A 2011 Carnegie Mellon news story says Anderson led the team that built an intelligent algebra tutor for high school students and that Carnegie Learning was spun off to sell the tutors. Carnegie Learning's timeline says it was founded in 1998 by Carnegie Mellon scientists working with Pittsburgh Public Schools math educators to launch Cognitive Tutor, and that MATHia (2010-2011) is the next generation of Cognitive Tutor. No source says how much of today's MATHia code or content dates from the NSF-funded work. Other Anderson awards in this group (8318629, 8470337, 8617083, 0087396) are earlier or research-only and are not listed.
