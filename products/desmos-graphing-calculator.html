Desmos Graphing Calculator

A free online graphing calculator where students plot points, graph curves and evaluate functions, with an audio trace feature for exploring graphs through sound.

  • Grades 6–8
  • Free, no login
  • Internet

Before you use it

  • The calculator is free to use in a browser. You need a free Desmos account only to save graphs.
  • Students use it in a web browser; the saved pages don't say whether each student needs a device. In the studies below, eighth graders worked on the task in groups of four or five.
  • Plan how you will frame the tool: in one study, students who didn't know how to use Desmos needed an extra tutorial from their teacher before they could graph.
  • This card is the calculator only. The ready-made Desmos lessons now live on Amplify Classroom (see that card).

What a teacher gets

The Desmos Graphing Calculator runs in the browser at desmos.com. Students type coordinates, expressions and equations into an expression list and see them graphed; they can evaluate a function with function notation or a function table, tap a curve to see intercepts and intersections, and pan and zoom the graph. Audio trace, an accessibility feature, lets a student explore an equation or a system of equations through sound. Graphs can be saved with a free account and shared as a link or an image.

Since 2022 the calculators have been run by Desmos Studio, a public benefit corporation led by founder Eli Luberoff; the classroom activities went to Amplify and are listed separately. Desmos Studio says the calculators stay free to teachers and students, and it licenses them to companies, so the same calculator appears on state tests and inside curriculum programs. Two peer-reviewed PME-NA papers by Stanford researchers, both from one district partnership and the same task (the Washing Machine Problem), study how eighth-grade teachers and students used Desmos (listed below).

Who made it

Desmos Studio PBC (the calculators part of Desmos, separate from Amplify since 2022). Developed by Eli Luberoff.

Research behind it

  1. Sustaining Cognitive Demand with Desmos TechnologyVictoria Delaney, Gina Kinsey (Stanford University). Proceedings of the 43rd Annual Meeting of the North American Chapter of the International Group for the Psychology of Mathematics Education (PME-NA), pp. 1764–1768, 2021. Teacher perceptions or use.Video analysis of one eighth-grade teacher's launch of a high-demand task (the Washing Machine Problem, 2016–17) and its enactment by three groups of four or five students using Desmos. The authors report that technology may raise cognitive demand, with teacher actions and students' familiarity with the technology also playing a role; two of the three groups used Desmos's features to raise demand.Proceedings front matter: "Each paper was reviewed by multiple referees in an anonymous review process"; overall acceptance rate 79%. Both authors at Stanford University. The paper prints no funding or conflict-of-interest statement. Neither author appears in the saved Desmos Fellows lists (cohorts 1–4).
  2. The Teacher's Role in Sustaining Cognitive Demand with DesmosVictoria Delaney (Stanford University). Proceedings of the 44th Annual Meeting of the North American Chapter of the International Group for the Psychology of Mathematics Education (PME-NA), pp. 1977–1981, 2022. Teacher perceptions or use.Video analysis of two eighth-grade teachers' launches and six student small groups using Desmos on the Washing Machine Problem (Spring 2017). The teachers' launches had less effect on students' cognitive demand and Desmos use than their later small-group interactions, including how they related Desmos to the task's goals.Proceedings front matter: "Each paper was reviewed by multiple referees in a double-blind process"; overall acceptance rate 66%. Single author at Stanford University. The paper prints no funding or conflict-of-interest statement. The author doesn't appear in the saved Desmos Fellows lists (cohorts 1–4). Same district partnership, task and first teacher's recordings as the 2021 paper, so the two papers are not independent studies.

Each study is about this product, by researchers with no tie to its developer we could find.

Who funded it

NSF did not fund this product. It is listed because independent researchers studied it in peer-reviewed venues (see Research behind it). No saved source names a funder. Desmos Studio is a public benefit corporation that keeps the calculators free to teachers and students and licenses them to companies for use in their products.
