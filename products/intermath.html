Before you use it

  • The original site is offline: intermath.coe.uga.edu did not resolve on Oct 30, 2026, and the last good Internet Archive capture of its home page is from February 2018. You can still read the investigations through the archive's copies, but the sample lesson-plan links were empty in the saved capture.
  • The investigations are written for teachers to work, not for students: the project says many would need changes for a middle school classroom and a few are not appropriate for middle school students at all.
  • You'll want dynamic geometry software. The Geometer's Sketchpad was InterMath's tool of choice, but the project says the problems should work in any software that lets you manipulate geometric objects; spreadsheets and graphing calculators also figure in.
  • The materials are old. The NSF award ran from 1999 to 2006, and the unit pages align to Georgia's older QCC and GPS standards and to NCTM standards.
  • The workshop and instructor support described on the site were for Georgia districts at the time; today what remains is the investigations, free and with no login.

What a teacher gets

InterMath was a University of Georgia professional development program for middle-grades teachers, built to deepen teachers' own understanding of the mathematics they teach through investigations supported by technology. Its website organized the investigations into four strands (Number Sense, Algebra, Geometry and Data Analysis) and 13 units, from fractions and decimals to functions and equations. Each unit had a short list of Recommended Investigations, chosen against seven criteria such as multiple solutions, multiple methods and an easy start, plus a longer list of Additional Investigations; the Functions & Equations recommendations include modifying slope and y-intercept and efficient fencing. The two unit pages we checked open by asking teachers how they would pose each problem to their own students.

In the workshops (about 50 hours of seat time; the NSF award planned 100), teachers worked investigations, completed a write-up for each (the problem, an initial strategy, how they actually approached it, and findings) and developed lesson plans. The site also held a student math dictionary, a Constructionary for building shapes in the software, and a small set of sample lesson plans.

Who made it

InterMath (University of Georgia). Developed by James W. Wilson, Michael J. Hannafin and Paul Ohme.

How these awards connect to this product

NSF award 9876611 ("Inter-Math", PI James W. Wilson, University of Georgia, 1999-2006) describes InterMath's workshops and follow-along support, and an InterMath article in The Mathematics Educator says grant ESI9876611 supported InterMath. Later NSF awards that mention InterMath (a study of its effects, an adaptation at another university) are research on or uses of the program, not its development, so they are not listed. The site also credits an earlier Eisenhower Plan Prototype Development Grant for early development.
