Before you use it

  • Heinemann sells the book with online access only to educational institutions: it is a restricted product bought on behalf of an educational institution through an Institutional account, so you'll likely need to order through your school or district. Cost (Heinemann, Oct 25, 2026): $43.33 list or $32.50 web/school price; printed on demand and nonreturnable. eBook and paperback editions are listed; it isn't stated whether they include the online sequences and videos.
  • Try it free first: Heinemann's sample includes one complete instructional sequence, Changing a Number in Addition or Subtraction, after a form asking for name, email, grade level, ZIP code and country.
  • Plan on Internet access: the lesson sequences and classroom videos are online, not in the printed book, which prints only two sequence overviews.
  • Time: each sequence runs as short sessions spread over a semester, according to the NSF project report. Session length isn't stated.
  • Grade fit: Heinemann lists kindergarten to grade 5, but the NSF project report says the model was built to help grade 2–5 teachers. The sequences work on whole-number operations, with one on fractions and one on decimals.

What a teacher gets

But Why Does It Work? (Heinemann, 2017; 176 pages, by Russell, Schifter, Bastable, Higgins and Kasman) presents a five-phase teaching model for mathematical argument in the elementary grades: noticing a regularity, articulating a claim, investigating it with representations such as manipulatives, diagrams or story contexts, constructing an argument for why it holds, and comparing one operation with another. Five chapters explain the model, what the teacher does in each phase and what students in the collaborating classrooms learned; chapters 1–4 include text and video examples, math investigations for the reader and focus questions. Heinemann says it is designed for individuals and study groups.

The book comes with eight online lesson sequences on addition, subtraction, multiplication and division with whole numbers, one also on fractions and one on decimals and powers of ten; two sequence overviews are printed as appendices. The NSF project report says each sequence runs as a series of short sessions over a semester, the way many teachers use classroom routines or Number Talks.
