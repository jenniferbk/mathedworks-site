Before you use it

  • This was written for university Calculus I and II at Arizona State. We found no high school version or pacing guide, so you'd be adapting a college text for AP or other high school calculus. The authors describe Chapters 1–4 as a forward-looking precalculus, Chapters 5–7 as Calculus 1 and Chapters 8–12 as Calculus 2.
  • You'll need a computer per student with Graphing Calculator (downloads from PacificT.com). The text links a free Mac App Store copy, a registered Mac or Windows download using a code from the instructor, a demo mode and a discounted Windows license. Desmos, GeoGebra or a TI/Casio calculator handle many simpler examples but not all.
  • Homework (exercises, activities and reflection questions) is programmed in iMathAS, a free open-source system. To use it outside ASU, your school would have to install iMathAS on its own server.
  • It's a work in progress, last revised August 2019. At least one section is marked "To Be Rewritten", and some Chapter 9 sections are PDFs rather than web pages.
  • Free to use and adapt under Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0.

Try one lesson

1.5 Constant rate of change patthompson.net

  • Grades not stated
  • Constant rate of change
  • Time not stated

Students study animations of y varying at a constant rate m with respect to x, so that dy = m dx, and answer reflection questions. Worked examples, such as Stan's drive to Tucson at 113 km/hr, show how to define a linear function from a constant rate and a starting value.

The text names no school grade: it was written for university calculus, and its authors call Chapters 1–4, which include this section, a forward-looking precalculus.

What a teacher gets

This open online textbook by Patrick Thompson, Mark Ashbrook and Fabio Milner restructures calculus around two linked questions: how fast a quantity varies at each moment, and how much of it there is. Twelve chapters run from infinitely small and large quantities, trigonometric functions and functions, variables and graphs, through rate of change, accumulation from rate of change, integration techniques, approximation and series, to polar coordinates and parametric functions. An instructor preface explains the design choices, such as distinguishing variables, parameters and constants and putting differentials at the center, and a glossary defines key terms.

The text grew out of Thompson's research and design experiments on students' calculus learning at Arizona State University. Students work extensively in the program Graphing Calculator, where the math statements they type are "live". The authors call it a work in progress; the latest revision is dated August 2019.

How these awards connect to this product

Both awards are the two halves of the collaborative NSF project "Project DIRACC" (Arizona State University and RMC Research Corporation). NSF funded part of the text: the ASU record says the project would redesign Calculus II and that the textbook it produces would be an open resource, and DIRACC's About page says the NSF proposal extended DIRACC to Calculus 2 and was funded in October 2016. The online text was first posted in July 2015, before the award, so the earliest Calculus I material predates NSF support; the extension to Calculus II and later revisions fall under the award. The RMC award's record does not say what RMC's part was. Thompson's earlier NSF awards on quantitative reasoning are not listed because no source ties them to this text.
