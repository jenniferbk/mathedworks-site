What NSF funding produced

NSF awards directly paid for 32 of the 55 products listed on this site, in whole or in part: 75 awards totaling $137.2 million, made from 1992 to 2021. Teachers can get those products today in a form NSF helped build.

For the other 23 products, NSF's role was further back. It funded an earlier edition, the research a product grew out of, or work the developers credit without naming an award. We report those connections separately below and don't add them into one total, because a dollar that built a product and a dollar that funded research decades before it are different things.

These figures link funding to products. They don't measure return on investment. Most of these awards also paid for research, teacher training and publications that aren't counted here. Many products also had funding from publishers, foundations or other agencies. And we list only the strongest products from a curated set of groups, not everything NSF funded.

Where do these numbers come from?

Dollar amounts and dates come straight from NSF's award records, filled in by script. Each amount is what NSF obligated to the award, or its estimated total where the record gives no obligated amount. Amounts are in the dollars of the year awarded, not adjusted for inflation. When one award paid for more than the listed product, the whole award still counts. For example, the $12.5 million MOBILIZE award behind Introduction to Data Science also produced other curricula.

Which products did NSF build directly?

These are products NSF awards paid to develop in the form teachers get today.

  • CODAP, a free online data analysis tool. Award 1435470, "Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP)," to the Concord Consortium, 2014–2017: $3.2 million. A 2013 award with the same title brings CODAP's total to $5.1 million.
  • Mathematical Knowledge for Teaching measures, from the Learning Mathematics for Teaching project at the University of Michigan. Award 0335411, 2003–2010: $4.3 million, one of six awards totaling $10.5 million.
  • FAACT fraction curriculum, free fraction activities and assessments for students with learning disabilities. Jessica Hunt's CAREER award, recorded under three numbers at three universities in turn (1253254, 1446250, 1708327), 2013–2021: about $1.0 million in all.
  • Think Math! and its programming extension, a K–5 curriculum from EDC. Award 0099093, 2001–2007: $5.1 million, plus two later awards for the programming work.

Which products did NSF fund in part?

For these products, NSF paid for some parts, versions or extensions, and other work produced the rest.

  • ASSISTments, a free web platform where students get instant feedback and hints and teachers see reports. Neil Heffernan's 2005 CAREER award (0448319, $646,075) describes building the system. The platform dates its start to 2003, before the award, and has had other federal and private funding.
  • DIRACC calculus, a free online calculus text. Award 1625678 (Pat Thompson and colleagues, 2016–2019, $810,939) extended it to Calculus II. The first Calculus I material was posted in 2015, before the award.
  • OGAP formative assessment. The Vermont Mathematics Partnership award (0227057, 2002–2012, $4.7 million) is the largest of four.

What did NSF fund in an earlier edition?

NSF funded the first editions of several of the best-known school curricula. Commercial publishers now sell later editions.

  • Interactive Mathematics Program, a four-year high school sequence. Award 9255262 (1992–1999): $9.4 million, one of three awards totaling $12.1 million.
  • Investigations in Number, Data, and Space, a K–5 curriculum from TERC. Award 9050210 (1990–2000): $7.0 million.
  • Core-Plus Mathematics, an integrated high school curriculum, now free from the authors as PDFs. Award 9255257 (1992–1998): $6.0 million, one of three awards totaling $16.3 million.
  • Everyday Mathematics, a PreK–6 curriculum. Award 9252984 (1992–1999): $5.4 million for the grades 4–6 materials.

What research did NSF fund that products grew from?

For these products, NSF funded the research or project behind them, not the product itself.

  • Children's Mathematics: Cognitively Guided Instruction. Award 8550236 (Carpenter, Fennema and colleagues, 1985–1990, $969,955) funded their research on applying cognitive science to math instruction, which preceded the book.
  • Rational Number Project fraction lessons. The earliest award on the site, 7920591 (Merlyn Behr, 1979–1982, $198,600), studied manipulatives and rational number learning. The project's free fraction lessons came out of research funded by five awards totaling $3.8 million.
  • Math Expressions, a PreK–6 program. Its publisher says it grew from Karen Fuson's NSF-funded Children's Math Worlds project. Award 9816320 (1999–2003): $999,987.

Where is the connection inferred?

For these products, the developers credit NSF without naming an award, or an award matches by title, people and years but no source ties it to the product. The listings say so plainly.

  • Pathways Precalculus. The publisher says two NSF grants funded Pathways research but gives no numbers. We identify one as Marilyn Carlson's Project Pathways award (0412537, 2004–2012, $13.4 million), from its title and investigator.
  • MATHia, Carnegie Learning's tutoring software. NSF funded John Anderson's intelligent tutors for algebra and geometry at Carnegie Mellon (8751890, 1988–1994, $1.8 million). Carnegie Learning was later founded to sell those tutors, but no source ties today's MATHia to a specific award.
  • PhET math simulations, free interactive simulations from the University of Colorado Boulder. Award 1503510 (2015–2020, $2.2 million) set out to build research-based simulations for algebraic thinking in grades 6–9. No source says which of today's math simulations came from it.

Which awards are the oldest and newest?

The oldest award is 7920591, from 1979: Merlyn Behr's study of manipulatives in learning rational numbers, at the start of the Rational Number Project. Its fraction lessons are still free to download from an archived copy of the project site.

The newest awards date from 2021. Award 2101382 ($3.3 million) funds M2Studio, the Concord Consortium's modeling workspace for high school students. Four collaborative awards that started in July 2021 (2100895, 2100903, 2100947 and 2100833, $3.0 million together) fund North Carolina's VISIONS project, whose NC2ML Discourse Moves kits are listed.
