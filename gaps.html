What's missing

Our listings are strongest on full curricula, units, toolkits and professional development materials for grades 3–8, in English. None of the listings is a stand-alone single lesson or task. Few are free and need no technology, a few math topics are thin, and early grades are thinner than middle grades. Every listing is in English. These gaps come partly from how we searched, so they don't show what NSF has or hasn't funded.

All counts below are from the 55 NSF-linked products listed by October 26, 2026. Illustrative Mathematics, added October 28, isn't counted; it covers grades 6–12 and has Spanish student materials for some courses. A product can carry several tags, so counts in a list can add up to more than 55.

What about other languages?

All 55 listings are in English. Five also come in Spanish: ASSISTments, Everyday Mathematics, Learning Trajectories, Math Expressions and Math Snacks. Three, CODAP, PhET and TinkerPlots, offer other languages. Only two listings have an NSF award whose title names English learners or Spanish speakers: the Fostering Geometric Thinking toolkit ("Fostering Mathematics Success in English Language Learners") and Math Expressions (Children's Math Worlds, "in English and in Spanish").

What about other kinds of products?

  • Teacher-knowledge instruments: 1 listing, the Mathematical Knowledge for Teaching measures. It requires an application and paid training.
  • Games: 4 listings (Cyberchase, Everyday Mathematics, Learning Trajectories and Math Snacks), mostly as one part of a larger product.
  • Recent work: only 2 listed products, NC2ML's Discourse Moves kits and M2Studio, rest entirely on NSF awards made since 2020. Grouped by the decade of each product's earliest NSF award, the 55 products break down as 1 from the 1970s, 5 from the 1980s, 13 from the 1990s, 18 from the 2000s, 16 from the 2010s and 2 from the 2020s.

We examined the full materials for only 1 of the 55 products. Most listings rest on sample materials (37) or the publisher's description alone (17), because we don't pay for access. Each listing shows which.

What kinds of NSF-funded work did our method probably miss?

Our agents started from well-known researchers and widely used products, then confirmed NSF awards. A later search of ten years of PME-NA conference papers added three products. That approach finds big, established projects. It probably misses:

  • Small and recent projects. Many NSF awards produce a set of tasks, a short unit or a video collection posted on a project page. These rarely show up in reviews, adoption lists or citation counts, which are the signals the agents leaned on. The PME-NA search found many active research groups whose products aren't out yet.
  • Products whose websites have gone dark. Several strong NSF projects were excluded because no current source could be found (see What didn't make it). Some of those materials may survive in archives or with former team members.
  • Work outside the education directorate's math programs. Computing education, informal learning (museums, television, after-school programs) and small-business awards fund math-related products. The agents caught some of these, such as Cyberchase, Bootstrap and Sketchpad, but didn't search those programs systematically.
  • Materials for particular groups of students. Neither shortlist agent's notes describe a search for materials for students with disabilities, English learners or bilingual classrooms. FAACT, built for students with learning disabilities, came in through Jessica Hunt, one of the starting names.
  • Products credited to NSF only inside the materials. When a product's web page doesn't mention NSF, we can't confirm the tie without the book or files, and several products were rejected on exactly that point.

What would fill these gaps?

  • Search NSF's award records directly, especially awards since 2015 in the DRK-12 program, for products named in project outcomes reports, rather than starting from reputation.
  • Search NCTM's practitioner journals and similar outlets for single tasks and lessons that credit an NSF award.
  • Run separate searches for Spanish-language and bilingual materials, materials for students with disabilities, and PreK–2.
  • Check web archives for products from projects whose sites have gone dark.
  • Ask researchers and teachers to suggest products, then put each suggestion through the same checks. Suggestions can go to mathedworks@gmail.com.
  • Have a person, with permission, look inside registration-walled and paid materials so more listings rest on the full materials.
